How to nail whitespace on your roofing website

Whitespace is not empty space — it is a design element that signals confidence, reduces cognitive load, and makes everything else on the page land harder

Why whitespace converts — the psychology
The core principle

Cramped = anxious. Spacious = confident. The brain reads whitespace as a signal about your business.

When a page feels dense and crowded, the brain interprets it as "this business is shouting at me, trying to overwhelm me into buying." When a page has generous space, the brain reads it as "this business is calm, established, and confident enough not to need to cram everything in." Your notes described it exactly: "Empty space is a psychological cue that tells the visitor: we are confident and don't need to shout." Hermès and Apple use extreme whitespace not to look minimal — they use it because spaciousness signals authority. You're selling a $10,000–$20,000 roofing job. Your page needs to feel like it costs that much.

Whitespace reduces cognitive load, which your notes called the #1 enemy of conversion. A brain that feels overwhelmed stops processing and leaves. A brain that feels calm keeps reading and converts.

The 8 whitespace rules — exact values for every situation
1
Section padding: 80–120px top and bottom on every section
This is the single most impactful spacing change. Most amateur sites use 40px. Premium sites use 80px minimum. The extra vertical breathing room is what makes the page feel curated rather than rushed. Double whatever you currently have and see how it looks.
padding: 96px 24px; /* desktop */ · padding: 64px 20px; /* mobile */
2
Hero section: 120px+ top padding — the most important section gets the most space
The hero is your 50-millisecond judge. It needs the most generous space of any section because it has to create an immediate sense of quality before anything is even read. The headline should feel like it has room to breathe, not like it's fighting for space.
padding: 120px 24px 96px; /* hero gets more top than bottom */
3
Breather sections (8 & 11): 60–80px top and bottom — the most spacious sections on the page
These sections work because they feel like relief after dense content. If they're cramped, they fail completely. The content should feel like it's floating in the middle of dark space — not pressed against the edges. This spaciousness IS the psychology — don't fight it.
padding: 72px 32px; /* desktop */ · padding: 56px 24px; /* mobile */
4
Between cards and elements: 16–24px gap minimum — never less than 16px
Cards touching or nearly touching look like they were placed by someone who ran out of space. 24px is the standard gap between all cards, service tiles, review blocks, and pricing cards. Use CSS gap on grid/flex containers — never margins on individual cards.
gap: 24px; /* grid/flex containers */ · gap: 16px; /* tight mobile grids */
5
Inside CTA buttons: 12–14px top/bottom · 24–28px left/right — never tighter
A cramped button looks like it's apologising for existing. A spacious button looks confident and demands to be clicked. The padding inside the button is what makes it feel important. If your CTA button looks like a text link with a box around it, the padding is too small.
padding: 13px 26px; /* primary CTA */ · padding: 11px 22px; /* secondary */
6
Line height: 1.7 for body text · 1.3–1.4 for headlines · 1.5 for subheadlines
Line height IS whitespace — it's the space between each line of text. Default line-height (1.2) makes text feel crammed together. 1.7 on body text is the standard for premium sites. It makes copy dramatically easier to read and feels inherently more expensive.
body: line-height:1.7 · h1/h2: line-height:1.3 · p.subhead: line-height:1.5
7
Between headline and paragraph: 12–16px · between paragraph and button: 24–32px
The space between elements within a section creates visual grouping. Headline and its paragraph should be close (12px) — they belong together. Paragraph and CTA button should be further apart (24px+) — the gap signals "this is a new action, not continuation of the text."
h→p: margin-bottom:14px · p→btn: margin-bottom:28px · btn→trust: margin-top:12px
8
Max content width: 1200px container · text blocks max 680px wide — never full bleed text
Full-width text on large screens creates lines of 100+ characters — impossible to read comfortably. Constrain your content to a max-width container and constrain text blocks further to 680px. The horizontal whitespace on either side is not wasted — it's the frame that makes the content feel intentional.
max-width:1200px; margin:0 auto; · .text-block { max-width:680px; }

Why use a spacing scale and not arbitrary values?

When every element uses a value from the same scale (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 120), the page achieves visual harmony automatically. Elements feel like they belong to the same system. When spacing is arbitrary (13px here, 19px there, 27px somewhere else), the page feels inconsistent even when the user can't articulate why. Define these as CSS custom properties once and reference them everywhere — changing one value updates every instance.

The single CSS line that makes the biggest difference to body text

Add this to your body selector and watch your entire site transform: body { line-height: 1.7; } — that one line is the fastest whitespace improvement you can make. It costs nothing, takes 10 seconds, and immediately makes every paragraph on the site feel more premium, more readable, and more trustworthy.

How to actually audit your current site — the 3-step process
Step 1 — the squint test

Squint at your site until it blurs — tight clusters are the problems

Open your website and literally squint until you can't read text. The page reduces to shapes and density. Any area that looks like a dense cluster of shapes is a whitespace problem. Any area that looks light and open is doing it right. Mark every cluster — those are your first fixes.

Your brain processes density before it processes content. Dense areas feel stressful even before a visitor has read a single word.
Step 2 — the double rule

Double every padding and margin value you currently have and compare

Open your CSS. Find every padding and margin value. Double all of them. Screenshot both versions side by side. In almost every case the doubled version will look more premium. Then scale back any that go too far — but your starting point is almost always too tight, not too loose.

Most people's instinct is to add more content when they have empty space. The correct instinct is to add more space when things feel cramped. Fighting this instinct is the entire skill.
Step 3 — the Hermès test

If it feels like too much space, you're close to right — trust the discomfort

When you increase whitespace to the correct amount, your first reaction will be "this feels like too much space, something is missing." That feeling is your amateur instinct fighting your professional decision. Luxury brands like Hermès and Apple use what feels like "too much space" to everyone who first encounters it. Let the discomfort exist for 24 hours. Come back to it. It will feel right. The visitor who arrives at your roofing site fresh — without the context of having designed it — will feel calm and confident, not empty. That's the goal.

You've been staring at your site for hours or days. You're immune to its density. A fresh visitor experiences it the way you experience a new site — the cramped one feels overwhelming, the spacious one feels trustworthy. Design for the fresh visitor, not for yourself.
