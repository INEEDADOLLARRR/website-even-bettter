Part 1 — video testimonials · the authenticity weapon
The most important rule — counterintuitive

Raw and unpolished beats professionally produced every single time

Your instinct will be to film a clean, well-lit, scripted testimonial. Don't. A homeowner standing in their kitchen or backyard, speaking naturally into their iPhone, is ten times more convincing than a polished studio video. Why? Because over-production looks like it was paid for. Roughness looks like truth. The visitor's brain has seen thousands of slick "customer testimonials" in ads — it filters them out automatically. A slightly shaky vertical phone video of a real person talking genuinely cannot be faked, and the brain knows it.

From your notes: "Over-produced testimonials look fake. Raw videos feel authentic and prove the person is real."
Format

Vertical smartphone video · 30–60 seconds

Ask the customer to film vertically on their phone — the same format as Instagram Stories and TikTok. This immediately looks organic, not corporate. 30–60 seconds is the sweet spot. Long enough to be credible, short enough to be watched to the end.

Never ask them to film horizontally in a studio setup — it kills the authenticity that makes video testimonials work.
Setting

Film outside in front of their newly finished roof

The absolute best setting is the customer standing outside their house with the completed roof visible behind them. This is a single shot that contains the person, the location, and the proof of the finished job. It eliminates every doubt in one frame.

The roof in the background is the evidence. The homeowner in the foreground is the validator. Together they are the most convincing possible image.
How to actually get video testimonials — the exact approach

The #1 reason roofers don't have video testimonials

They never ask. Or they ask at the wrong moment. The perfect moment to ask is right when you hand over the completion paperwork or do the final walkthrough — the homeowner is at peak satisfaction, relieved the job is done, and looking at a beautiful new roof. That emotional high is when "sure, I'll record something" comes most naturally. Waiting a week means the feeling fades and the request feels like an inconvenience.

The exact ask — word for word

What to say to get a yes every time

Don't ask "could you leave us a review?" That's vague and puts all the work on them. Instead give them the exact format, the exact duration, and make it feel effortless. Say it in person, right at job completion.

The script
"[Name], I'm so glad you're happy with how it turned out. Would you do me a huge favor? Just stand right here in front of the roof and record a 30-second video on your phone — just say what you thought of the process and whether you'd recommend us. It doesn't need to be perfect at all. I'll send you a reminder text tonight so you don't forget. It genuinely helps us more than anything else."
Key phrases: "huge favor" (makes them feel generous, not obligated), "doesn't need to be perfect" (removes the barrier of filming anxiety), "reminder text" (follows up without being pushy).
Anatomy of a perfect video testimonial — what it should contain
Opening (0–5s)
Who they are and where they live — unprompted. This establishes they are a real local person.
"Hi, I'm Sarah, I live in Oak Park and I just had my roof replaced by [Company]…"
The problem (5–15s)
What the situation was before. The worse the problem, the more powerful the testimonial.
"We had a massive leak after the storm last month — water was coming through the ceiling in two rooms…"
The experience (15–40s)
What it was like working with the company — speed, communication, professionalism, cleanliness.
"They came the same day I called, gave me a quote the next morning, and the whole team was so clean and professional. They even swept the driveway…"
The outcome (40–55s)
The result and emotional payoff. This is the line that resonates with other homeowners.
"I haven't had a single problem since and I feel so much better knowing my house is protected. I'd recommend them to anyone in the area."
Visual proof
Film them turning around briefly to show the finished roof behind them. Even 3 seconds of this is worth more than 30 seconds of talking.
Customer turns, points at roof: "And there it is — looks incredible."
Part 2 — text reviews · the quick-scanner's trust signal
Why you need both video AND text

Video proves the person is real. Text allows quick scanning. You need both.

Different visitors consume social proof differently. Some will watch a video testimonial start to finish. Most will scan text reviews in 10 seconds. Video and text together cover both types of visitor. Your notes specifically said: "The video proves the client is real, while the text allows for quick scanning." Running only one format means you're converting only one type of visitor.

The combination also has a compounding effect — a visitor who watches a video and then reads three matching text reviews has their trust doubled, not just added to.
Anatomy of a perfect text review — what makes one convert
Real first name + last initial
Never "Anonymous" or "Happy Customer." First name and last initial is the minimum. First and last name is better.
"Sarah M." or "Sarah Mitchell" — never "A satisfied customer"
Neighborhood + job type
The location and what was done. This makes it local and specific — the two things a roofing review must be.
"Oak Park · Full roof replacement · June 2024"
5 stars + platform logo
The platform (Google, Yelp) is the third-party validator. The stars are the instant visual signal. Both must be visible without reading.
Google logo + ★★★★★ — visible at a glance before reading the text
Specific quote — 1–3 sentences
The best reviews are specific about a problem solved, a speed exceeded, or a fear removed. Generic praise ("great company!") is weak. Specific detail is strong.
"Called at 8am after the storm, they were at my house by noon. Entire repair done same day."
Verifiable link
If the review is from Google or Yelp, link to it. Visitors who want to verify can — and the fact that you link to it signals you have nothing to hide.
Small "View on Google" link below the review text
Good review vs weak review — what actually converts
Weak — barely converts
"Great service! Very professional and did a good job. Would recommend to others."
— John D.
No location, no job type, no specific detail, no problem solved. Could be written by anyone about anything. The brain files it as generic noise.
Strong — converts hard
"Called at 8am after the storm. They were at my house by noon and the repair was done same day. Swept the entire driveway before they left. I've used three roofers over the years — none came close."
— Mike T. · Downtown [City] · Emergency repair · March 2024
Specific timeframes, specific action (driveway sweep), comparison to competitors, real name and neighborhood. Every line removes a different doubt.
How many reviews to show — and which ones to pick
Quantity

2 video testimonials + 4–6 text reviews minimum

Two videos give you variety without overwhelming. Four to six text reviews is enough to feel like proof, not so many it becomes a wall of text. If you have more, add a "See all reviews" link to your Google profile — don't stuff the page.

The goal is overwhelming evidence, not an exhaustive list. Quality and specificity beat quantity every time.
Which reviews to pick

Choose reviews that each kill a different doubt

Pick reviews that collectively cover: speed of response, cleanliness, insurance handling, price fairness, communication, and quality of the final result. Each review kills a different fear. Together they cover every objection a homeowner has.

Don't pick 5 reviews that all say "great job!" Pick 5 reviews that each tell a different story about a different fear removed.
The section headline — don't call it "Testimonials"
Headline options ranked best to worst

The headline must signal that these are real, local, verifiable people

The word "Testimonials" is the weakest possible headline — it sounds like a marketing section. The headline should prime the visitor to trust what follows before they read a single word.

Weak: "Customer Testimonials" — sounds like a marketing section

The "no review carousel" rule — from your notes

Auto-rotating carousels feel like the company chose what you see and when. Static reviews that the visitor can scroll through at their own pace feel like you have nothing to hide. Your notes consistently emphasised reducing friction and giving the visitor control. A carousel takes control away. Show all reviews visible at once, or in a simple scrollable row the visitor navigates manually.
