The 6 photo types you need — shoot all of these
Photo type 1 — most important

Before & after of the same roof

Same angle, same time of day, same framing. The contrast does all the selling for you. Shoot from the street at eye level so it looks like what the homeowner will see pulling into their driveway.

Why: Side-by-side before/after is the single most convincing visual proof of competence — no copy needed.

Tip: Shoot the "after" while the crew is still there — the light is the same and you won't forget.

Photo type 2

Crew actively working on a job

Candid shots of the crew mid-work — installing shingles, on the roof, using equipment. Everyone in branded shirts with the company logo visible. Action shots feel more authentic than posed ones.

Why: Proves a real crew exists. "Cowboy contractors" don't have branded uniforms or real teams on site.

Tip: Shoot from slightly below — makes the crew look competent and the roof look impressive.

Photo type 3

Smiling homeowner in context

The homeowner standing in front of their finished roof or shaking hands with your rep. Get their permission and use their first name and street/neighborhood in the caption. Never use a stock "happy customer."

Why: A real local face with a real name is the most verifiable trust signal possible. Visitors think "I could find this person."

Tip: Ask right when you hand them the completion paperwork — they're happiest in that moment.

Photo type 4

Branded company truck at a job site

The truck parked in front of a completed job — clean, branded, with the company name and number clearly visible. This is the exact visual the homeowner will see pulling up to their house on job day.

Why: A branded truck signals an established business. It also pre-answers "what will it look like when they arrive?"

Tip: Park it so the house and roof are visible in the background — one shot, two proof points.

Photo type 5

Material close-ups and detail shots

Tight shots of installed shingles, flashing, ridge caps, or gutters. Show the quality of the work up close. Your notes specifically mentioned showing the product at "4× zoom" to make material and craftsmanship visible.

Why: Homeowners can't evaluate roofing quality from street level. Close-ups prove the finish work is clean and professional.

Tip: Shoot in direct sunlight — it shows texture and depth that flat light hides.

Photo type 6

Leadership team / owner headshot

A real photo of the owner or key team members — not a group shot in front of an office. Outdoors at a job site is ideal. This person will answer the phone when the homeowner calls. Putting a real face on it matters enormously.

Why: People buy from people. A named, real owner signals accountability — they can't disappear if their face is on the website.

Tip: Use the same photo in the "why choose us" section and the footer for recognition.

The rule behind the alternating grid

Your notes said "uniform grids feel monotonous — variation keeps attention and makes scanning easier." A grid where every photo is the same size looks like a stock gallery. An alternating grid — some large, some small, some wide — feels curated and intentional, which your brain reads as professional. It also lets you make the most powerful photos (before/after, crew action) larger, and supporting evidence (close-ups, truck) smaller.

Why metrics go here, not just the trust strip

Repeating the numbers next to real photo evidence doubles their impact. The photos are the evidence. The number is the conclusion.

The neighborhood copy rule — prove you're truly local
The local legitimacy signal

Caption every photo with a real neighborhood name

Under each photo, add a line like: "Oak Park, [City] · Installed July 2024" or "Completed in Riverside Heights — full replacement after storm damage." Mentioning specific local areas — streets, neighborhoods, suburbs — is what makes a visitor think "these people actually work near me" instead of "this could be anyone anywhere."

Why: Your notes said "neighborhood context proves you are local and legit." A homeowner in Oak Park who sees "Oak Park, 3 jobs this summer" is not calling anyone else.

The "show what you want to grow" principle — from your notes

Your notes said explicitly: "Don't just upload pictures of every tiny repair job you do, unless that's all you want to attract." If you want full replacements at $15,000–$20,000, show full replacements. If you want emergency repairs, show emergency repairs. The type of work you photograph is the type of client you will attract. Curate the gallery to match your most profitable and desirable jobs — not just whatever photos you happen to have on your phone.
