Part 1 — the trust strip (review widget)
Element 1

5-star Google or Yelp badge

Show the platform logo (Google G or Yelp burst), the 5 stars in gold, and your overall rating (e.g. "4.9/5"). Link it to your live Google Business profile so visitors can verify it instantly.

Why: The platform logo is the trust signal — it says a third party verified this, not just you.
Element 2

Short real quote + real name

Pick one short punchy quote (max 10–12 words) from a real review. Show the customer's first name and last initial, plus their neighborhood. Example: "Fixed our leak same day!" — Sarah M., Oak Park.

Why: A name and neighborhood makes it verifiable — visitors know they could look this person up.
Critical rule for the widget

Make it look native — not designed

The widget should look like it was pulled straight from Google — not a heavily styled banner with gradients and custom fonts. A plain card with the Google logo, stars, quote, and name is more convincing than a polished custom graphic. The more it looks like a real embed, the more the brain trusts it automatically.

Why: Heavily designed "testimonial banners" look fake. Native widgets look real. You want cold traffic to think "oh that's actually their Google rating."
Part 2 — the authority stack (credentials & badges)
Badge 1 — most important

Contractor license number

Display it plainly: "Licensed · #CR12345" or "State License: CR-12345." Don't hide it. In the US market, an unlicensed contractor is the homeowner's biggest fear — showing the number immediately disarms that.

Why: It's verifiable. Anyone can Google your license number and confirm you're real. That's exactly why it converts.
Badge 2

Insurance status

A simple line or badge: "Fully Insured · General Liability + Workers' Comp." You don't need to show policy numbers — just the confirmation that both types of insurance are active.

Why: If a worker gets hurt on their roof and you're not insured, the homeowner is liable. This removes that fear completely.
Badge 3

Industry certifications

GAF Master Elite, Owens Corning Preferred Contractor, BBB Accredited, or any manufacturer certification. Show the actual logo — not just text. If you have multiple, show them all in a small row.

Why: These are third-party endorsements. They signal you met someone else's standard — not just your own claim.
Badge 4

Years in business + project count

Two numbers side by side: "20 Years in [City] · 500+ Roofs Completed." Keep it factual and conservative — never round up aggressively. "500+" is more believable than "thousands."

Why: Numbers are scannable and concrete. "20 years" tells the brain "this business survived long enough to build real experience."
Badge 5 — optional but powerful

Warranty badge

If you offer a workmanship warranty (e.g. "10-Year Workmanship Guarantee"), make it a badge. Pair it with the manufacturer's material warranty if applicable (e.g. "25-Year GAF Shingle Warranty"). This is often the single element that tips a hesitant homeowner into calling.

Why: A roof is a $10,000–$20,000 decision. A warranty reduces perceived risk to near zero. It answers "what if something goes wrong?" before they ask it.
Do this / not that
Do this
  • Make it visible above the fold
  • Use the real Google/Yelp logo
  • Show a real customer name + neighborhood
  • Display the actual license number
  • Keep it in a single compact horizontal strip
  • Link the rating to your live Google profile
  • Use the real certification logos (GAF, BBB)
Never do this
  • Use generic star graphics without a platform logo
  • Show "Anonymous Customer" on any review
  • Skip the license number — it's your #1 trust signal
  • Make it a huge designed banner — keep it subtle
  • Show fake or inflated stats ("1000s of happy clients")
  • Put this below the fold — it must be seen immediately

The placement rule — why "above the fold" is non-negotiable

Your notes stated that users form a judgment in 50 milliseconds and that trust signals must be visible before the visitor has any doubts. The moment someone arrives from a Google search, they're a cold stranger. They haven't decided to trust you yet. The trust strip does one job: convert that stranger into a warm lead before they scroll a single pixel. If it's below the fold, it's too late for most visitors — they've already bounced or formed their opinion.
